Four of 10 Steinbach council candidates attend election forum

By: Adi Kapoor Posted: 11:45 PM CDT Wednesday, Oct. 21, 2026

Six candidates running for the Steinbach city council failed to show up at the election forum organized by South East Equity Coalition Centre ahead of municipal elections. The forum was held at the Southeast Event Centre on Oct. 7 and was only attended by four of the 10 council candidates. There are six council seats available.

Candidates Mitesh Kumar, Brad Unger, Gary Dyck and Nicholas Goertzen participated in the forum. Candidates Greg Carlos, Ron Falk, Bill Hiebert, Jake Hiebert, Damian Penner and Susan Penner were not present.

The forum started with attendees divided into groups to discuss life quality, housing and development, health and well-being, and transportation. Later, the candidates spoke on the issues that were raised in these conversations.

The co-organizers Gary Snider and Gay Boese said they planned the forum for months.

“It was an opportunity for the community to be heard, and for the candidates to hear the community,” Boese said, noting she began reaching out to the candidates from the beginning of September.

“Every candidate got two emails, two phone calls as a minimum, ” she said.

Boese noted some candidates said they had prior commitments, while some did not give any reason for not accepting the forum invitation.

“I really feel it’s their loss because they missed an opportunity to hear from the people, ” she said.

“You can sit down with the mayor and talk, but to actually engage with councillors, it’s hard,” Boese said. “They come to our doors occasionally, when they want our vote. Otherwise, there are four years where we don’t see them.”

Boese added that close to 100 people attended the event and all four candidates who showed up gave her positive feedback on the forum.

“All of them were very grateful. They thought it was a wonderful event,” she said.

Attendees John and Kiran Mahi, who have lived in Steinbach for 40 years, said discussions on quality of life and health were the most important to them.

“We would like to see a good arts centre and more recreation for young people, ” John said.

John and Kiran said they would also like to see more jobs to keep the young people in Steinbach.

The municipal elections will be held on Wednesday, Oct. 28.

Steinbach non-profits are raising $32,000 to provide turkeys or chickens to local families for Christmas. COLUMN: On Parliament Hill – Why small businesses matter

Ted Falk, MP for Provencher 4 minute read Yesterday at 4:21 PM CDT

Some of you may know the story of Canadian entrepreneur Tobi Lütke. A German-born immigrant, Tobi came to Canada to pursue a software development apprenticeship. In the early 2000s, he started an online snowboard shop. Frustrated by the lack of e-commerce software, he created his own platform. Shopify was founded in 2006 and is now widely regarded as one of the world’s leading e-commerce platforms.

A society that encourages new business, innovation, and entrepreneurial creativity benefits. Customer choice increases as more businesses enter the market. New businesses can develop creative solutions to local problems, introduce new products, services, and technologies. Small businesses often reflect local culture, values, and traditions. Supporting entrepreneurship encourages initiative, skill development, and opportunities. What’s more, small businesses create new jobs, generate new wealth, and build economic resilience.

A country that discourages entrepreneurship and business growth has the opposite effect.

In a survey, Canadian businesses responded to the Canadian Federation of Independent Businesses survey identifying the high cost of doing business in Canada as the biggest deterrent for not starting a business. High costs, taxes and red tape discourage entrepreneurship.

COLUMN: Think Again – Convicted murderer doesn’t belong on campus

Michael Zwaagstra 4 minute read Yesterday at 4:14 PM CDT

Imagine a young woman attending a university law class. How do you think she will feel when she suddenly learns that the man sitting beside her in class for the last few weeks is a convicted murderer?

This isn’t a hypothetical scenario. For the entire month of September and part of October, a convicted murderer attended law classes at McGill University. The law student in question is Jeffrey George Ewert, a man convicted over 40 years ago of second-degree murder and attempted murder.

In 1984, when he was in his 20s, Ewert sexually assaulted a young woman and strangled her to death. Six weeks later, he attacked a fifteen-year-old girl who rejected his advances. After severely beating and choking her, he dumped her body in a ditch. While the girl survived the assault, she was left blind and paralyzed.

For these heinous crimes, Ewert received a life sentence. However, a life sentence in Canada doesn’t necessarily mean life behind bars. Even the most heinous criminals can apply for parole after enough time has passed. In Ewert’s case, the parole board granted him day parole last year. This made it possible for Ewert to attend law classes at McGill.

COLUMN: Tales from the Gravel Ridge – Learning to listen to our lives

Maria Falk Lodge 4 minute read Preview

COLUMN: Tales from the Gravel Ridge – Learning to listen to our lives

Maria Falk Lodge 4 minute read Wednesday, Oct. 21, 2026

For Canadians living on the prairies the month of October is a reminder that summer is winding down. Needless to say, this also applies to our entire country. In our area, however, the reality of the coming of colder weather, and the likelihood of snow decking our fields soon, cannot be ignored. By failing to take note of the signs of impending change all around us, we may well miss the joy and beauty of the season. Surely that also includes the gifts others bestow on us in our day to day interactions.

For me personally, memories of harvest arise in a variety of scenarios. How could I fail to remember stooking at the Goritz farm, situated across the road from what was then named the Hanover School. I vividly recall the experience where, under our father’s supervision and assistance we arranged sheaves of grain in the shape of a pyramid. What a beautiful sight a field of stooked sheaves in their golden glory was back in the day.

A most memorable detail of that particular income-earning experience was the meal we were served that day so long ago. In total we were a team of five, my father, Cornelius Falk, along with my older sister Anne, and my younger brothers, David and Ernie, and I. At noon, all of us were invited to sit down at the kitchen table, whereupon Mrs. Goritz spoke a prayer and then served us a wonderful meal. Imagine the impression that meal must have made on us if I remember it so many decades later.

On another occasion we also did some stooking for Abram and Maria Dueck whose farm was a short distance down the road from the Goritz farm. I recall that Mrs. Dueck graciously served us “Faspa” that quintessential afternoon meal among Mennonites of that era. Such a meal invariably called for coffee and something in the category of home-baked bread, accompanied by butter and other condiments, and possibly also some more dainty fare. I wonder how Mrs. Dueck must have felt when she observed the capacity that children and young adults have for practically inhaling the food she served us that day, especially in light of the fact that there were no children in their household.

COLUMN: On the Fence Line – Elevating the equestrian: Building a better future for horses and riders

Manitoba Horse Council 3 minute read Wednesday, Oct. 21, 2026

A healthy equestrian community takes more than good riding skills. It depends on understanding our horses, supporting one another, managing stress and creating barn and competition environments where everyone feels safe and respected.

On Nov. 7, equine owners, riders, coaches, boarders, parents and others in Manitoba’s horse community are invited to Elevating the Equestrian at Sport Manitoba in Winnipeg. This special event combines an afternoon of expert presentations with an evening keynote and celebration, offering practical knowledge to improve both equestrian experiences and community culture.

The afternoon program runs from 12:30 p.m. to 4:45 p.m. and covers topics including recognizing toxic barn culture, preparing for infectious disease outbreaks, communicating more effectively with horses, navigating perimenopause and managing nerves and emotions.

Presenters bring expertise in equine behaviour, veterinary medicine, sport psychology and stress management. Speakers include equine behaviour specialist and judge Lindsay Grice; veterinarian and equine surgeon Dr. Chris Bell; veterinary behaviour specialist Dr. Karen Machin; psychologist and performance expert Dr. Lindsay Arnal Wishnowski; and somatic practitioner Joy Onyschak.

COLUMN: View from the Legislature – Separation votes cause long and short-term pain

Kelvin Goertzen, MLA for Steinbach 3 minute read Wednesday, Oct. 21, 2026

On Oct. 19 there will be a referendum vote in Alberta that asks residents of that province a number of questions, the most prominent being whether they wish to begin a process of negotiating separation from Canada. While Canada has for many years heard the rumblings, sometimes louder sometimes softer, from Quebec about sovereignty, the Alberta vote is something different and seemingly motivated by different factors.

While polling would indicate that the Alberta separation question vote is likely to be defeated, the fact that it is being held signals both current and longer-term concerns. Unlike Quebec, whose past referendums largely focused on cultural and language issues, the Alberta vote has its roots in economic factors. The long-standing concern that the federal government has been at best unhelpful and at worst obstructionist in helping Alberta get natural resources like oil to market through the construction of pipelines is at the heart of the referendum. And while the current Liberal prime minister has made significant overtures, although not yet progress, when it comes to new pipelines, a portion of Alberta’s population has lost trust in our federal system of government.

And yet, like most Canadians, polling shows that most Albertans feel that Canada is better united and that there remains, despite challenges, a great deal of promise in our country. But as former Alberta Premier Jason Kenney points out, none of it matters if people don’t get out and vote in Alberta to keep the country united. But even if the expected vote to remain in Canada materializes, there is short and long-term economic pain caused by these votes. It not only causes uncertainty, but it raises the concern of future votes as it becomes a normalized political practice.

Such is the case in Quebec, where the talk of separation has been mostly on the backburner since the razor thin vote of 1995. That vote had measurable negative impact on the Quebec economy and undoubtedly resulted in some business investment chill given how close the country came to potentially separating. Now, a new PQ government has just been elected which ran on having another sovereignty referendum, albeit not until Donald Trump is no longer President.

COLUMN: Village News – Setting things right: Mennonite bonesetters and community healing

Garth Doerksen 5 minute read Preview

COLUMN: Village News – Setting things right: Mennonite bonesetters and community healing

Garth Doerksen 5 minute read Wednesday, Oct. 21, 2026

For generations, Mennonite communities relied on local healers known as trajcht’moakes, a Low German term meaning “those who set things right.” Long before doctors and hospitals were readily available in rural Manitoba, these bonesetters provided essential health care for families facing injuries, accidents, and illness. They treated broken bones, dislocations, strained muscles, and painful joints, often traveling great distances to reach those in need. Their knowledge and skills were learned through observation, experience, and years of practical work, with skills often passed from one generation to the next.

In isolated farming communities, bonesetters occupied a place of deep respect and trust. A successful treatment could restore a farmer’s ability to work, help a child walk again, or bring relief to someone suffering chronic pain. Their reputation spread from neighbour to neighbour, and patients often returned repeatedly because of the confidence they placed in these healers’ hands.

One of the most remarkable of these community healers was Gertrude Klassen (1892–1986), widely known as Tante Trutje or Klosis Trutje. Although respected as a chiropractor and bonesetter, her legacy extends far beyond physical healing. Klassen devoted her life to caring for vulnerable children, combining practical medical skills with extraordinary compassion. Over a span of more than forty years, she welcomed fifty-three children into her home. Some were newborns, others were seriously ill, and many came from difficult family circumstances.

Her first long-term patient was a nephew disabled by polio. Through dedicated care and treatment, she helped restore his mobility. As the years passed, her home became a refuge where children found stability, care, and opportunity. For many families, Gertrude Klassen provided not only healing hands but also a loving home when they needed it most.
